# Israel 2026 Knesset Election: Simulation Briefing

Compiled on Friday, 23 October 2026, four days before the vote, from public reporting (sources at the end). Figures are as reported by the cited outlets. Individual polls carry margins of error of roughly ±4 percentage points, which means ±1–2 seats for a single party.

## 1. The election at a glance

- **Election day:** Tuesday, 27 October 2026. Polling stations close at 22:00.
- **What is at stake:** all 120 seats of the 26th Knesset. A government needs the support of at least 61 members.
- **System:** nationwide proportional representation with closed party lists. A list must win 3.25% of valid votes (about four seats) to enter the Knesset. Leftover seats are divided using the Bader–Ofer (D'Hondt) method. Two lists can sign a surplus-vote agreement so they compete for leftover seats as if they were one list.
- **Why now:** the 25th Knesset dissolved itself on 17 July 2026 after serving its full four-year term. It was the first Knesset since 1988 to complete a full term without an early dissolution.
- **Context:** this is the first general election since Hamas's 7 October 2023 attack, the Gaza war that followed, and the wars with Hezbollah in Lebanon and with Iran. Many Israelis and outside analysts treat it as a referendum on whether Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu should stay in power.
- **Lists:** 38 lists were submitted. Benny Gantz's Blue and White withdrew on 19 October after polling below the threshold. It still appears on the ballot, but votes cast for it will be wasted.
- **Poll blackout:** no new opinion polls may be published from 23:59 on Friday 23 October until polls close on 27 October. The polls in section 4 are the last public data before the vote.
- **After the vote:** the President consults the parties and gives the mandate to the Knesset member he believes is best placed to form a government. If that person fails within 28 days, the President can hand the mandate to someone else or to the Knesset. If nobody assembles 61 seats, Israel holds another election.
- **The 2022 baseline (25th Knesset):** Likud 32, Yesh Atid 24, Religious Zionism 14 (a joint list with Otzma Yehudit and Noam), National Unity 12, Shas 11, United Torah Judaism 7, Yisrael Beiteinu 6, Ra'am 5, Hadash–Ta'al 5, Labor 4. Netanyahu's bloc won 64 seats and formed the 37th government with Otzma Yehudit, Noam, the Religious Zionist Party, UTJ and Shas.

## 2. The blocs and the swing players

**Netanyahu bloc (the outgoing coalition):**
- Likud (Benjamin Netanyahu)
- Shas (Aryeh Deri)
- United Torah Judaism (Yitzhak Goldknopf)
- Religious Zionist Party–Zehut, a joint list led by Bezalel Smotrich
- Otzma Yehudit (Itamar Ben-Gvir)

Amcha Yisrael ("People of Israel"), led by Ofer Winter, is not part of the outgoing coalition but has pledged to sit only in a Netanyahu-led government.

The bloc has not been stable. Otzma Yehudit left the coalition in January 2025 over the Gaza ceasefire and returned two months later. UTJ left in July 2025 over the conscription of ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) men. Angered by Netanyahu, UTJ has reportedly said it will not oppose a government led by Gadi Eisenkot.

**Change bloc (the Zionist opposition):**
- Yashar! (Gadi Eisenkot)
- Together, also called B'Yachad (Naftali Bennett together with Yair Lapid)
- Yisrael Beiteinu (Avigdor Liberman)
- The Democrats (Yair Golan)

In September 2026 the change bloc's leaders signed a joint statement. They agreed not to attack each other, to establish a commission of inquiry into 7 October, to adopt a unified position on drafting ultra-Orthodox men, to impose term limits on prime ministers, and to draft a constitution. They have not agreed on who would be prime minister. The statement also takes no position on governing with Arab parties, and Netanyahu quickly attacked that omission. Eisenkot and almost every other Zionist opposition party have pledged not to sit in a coalition with Arab-majority parties.

**Arab-majority parties:** the Joint List (led by Yousef Jabareen; Ahmad Tibi is one of its best-known MKs) and Ra'am (Mansour Abbas). In seat counts both sit outside the Netanyahu camp, but the change bloc's pledges keep them outside its coalition plans. Ra'am has said it wants to join a future government and recognizes Israel as a Jewish state. Polls suggest Arab parties could win record support.

**Unaligned:** The Reservists and the Economic Party, led by Yoaz Hendel and Yaron Zelekha, refuse to place themselves in either bloc. They pledge not to sit with ultra-Orthodox or Arab-majority parties, and they draw mostly from the Netanyahu camp.

**The core arithmetic:** in the final week of polling, neither bloc reaches 61 seats except in Channel 14's polls. Analysts note the opposition cannot reach 61 without either the Haredi parties or Ra'am, and Netanyahu cannot without new partners. Three routes to a government are discussed:
1. A rotation agreement, for example Eisenkot and Netanyahu taking turns as prime minister.
2. A government that relies on an Arab party.
3. A government that brings the ultra-Orthodox parties across the bloc line.

Long negotiations, and their failure leading to a repeat election, are considered plausible.

## 3. Parties and leaders

**Likud.** Led by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, it was the largest party in 2022 with 32 seats. The final polls give it 18–21 seats in most surveys, 26 from i24 News/Direct Polls and 30 from Channel 14. US President Donald Trump endorsed Netanyahu on 18 October. A central Likud attack line is that the opposition will depend on Arab parties: "The government that you will establish with Abbas, Tibi, Yair Golan and Liberman. We won't let that happen." Netanyahu has avoided televised debates in past campaigns and was seen as unlikely to accept Eisenkot's challenge to one. Likud submitted a 35-person slate that includes nine Netanyahu appointees and only four women. It signed a surplus-vote agreement with RZP–Zehut. Netanyahu's corruption trial is still under way. A pardon request is pending with President Isaac Herzog, who deferred a decision and pushed for mediation toward a plea deal. Trump has publicly pressed for a pardon.

**Yashar!** Led by Gadi Eisenkot, former IDF chief of staff and a member of the war cabinet after 7 October (when Gantz's National Unity joined an emergency government), Yashar! is widely seen as Netanyahu's leading challenger. It is the largest party in most final polls, at 20–23 seats. Eisenkot frames the vote around 7 October: "On October 27, three years after the October 7 failure — the gravest failure in Israel's history — the people of Israel will choose a leader." He challenged Netanyahu to a televised debate, posting "What are you afraid of?". On 27 September he met families of hostages, hostages killed in captivity and 7 October victims at Hostages Square in Tel Aviv. He has received death threats. On 29 September the Shin Bet declined to give him a state security detail but allowed his private guards to carry weapons. Yashar! signed a surplus-vote agreement with The Democrats.

**Together (B'Yachad).** Headed by two former prime ministers, Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, it merges Bennett 2026 with Lapid's Yesh Atid. Final polls: 11–15 seats. Its voters are the least committed of any party Walla reported: only 23% are completely certain of their vote. Together signed a surplus-vote agreement with Yisrael Beiteinu.

**The Democrats.** Led by Yair Golan, former IDF deputy chief of staff, this is the merged Labor–Meretz list. Final polls: 8–10 seats. Golan received repeated death threats. The party petitioned the High Court of Justice for a Shin Bet security detail. After reviewing the state's alternative security measures, the Supreme Court President suggested the party withdraw the petition. On 19 October one of its billboards was defaced with "wanted dead or alive". The graffiti targeted the party's support for diplomatic engagement with the Palestinians and its willingness to govern with Arab parties. Golan replied that "hope will defeat hatred".

**Yisrael Beiteinu.** Led by Avigdor Liberman, a former finance minister, the party has a largely secular base that has historically been Russian-speaking. Liberman is a long-time advocate of drafting ultra-Orthodox men. Final polls: 5–9 seats. On 21 October the party filed a complaint over explicit death threats against Liberman. In a Maariv head-to-head, Netanyahu led Liberman 43% to 37%.

**Shas.** Led by Aryeh Deri, Shas is the Mizrahi ultra-Orthodox party. Final polls: 6–9 seats. It signed a surplus-vote agreement with UTJ on 11 October, and the two pledged to cooperate in the next Knesset. Observers expect Mizrahi voters angered by the Yinon Magal remarks (section 6) to drift toward Shas or Otzma Yehudit rather than toward the centrist opposition.

**United Torah Judaism (UTJ).** Led by Yitzhak Goldknopf, UTJ is the Ashkenazi ultra-Orthodox party. Final polls: 7–8 seats. It left the coalition in July 2025 over conscription and has reportedly said it will not block an Eisenkot-led government. Its ultimate coalition choice is one of the biggest unknowns.

**Religious Zionist Party–Zehut.** Led by Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, this national-religious joint list held its primary on 26 July. Final polls: 6–8 seats. It has a surplus-vote agreement with Likud.

**Otzma Yehudit.** Led by National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, the party sits on the far right and now runs separately from RZP. Final polls: 7–9 seats. One analyst described Ben-Gvir as wanting "to be a kingmaker on the right", with an eye on future elections as well as this one.

**Joint List.** An alliance of Arab-majority parties led by Yousef Jabareen. Final polls: 8–9 seats. It has the most committed voters of any party Walla reported: 81% are completely certain. It signed a surplus-vote agreement with Ra'am.

**Ra'am.** Led by Mansour Abbas, Ra'am is a conservative Islamist Arab party. It was the first Arab party to join a governing coalition, the Bennett–Lapid government of 2021–2022. It is running alone after holding primaries. Final polls: 5–6 seats. It has said it wants to join a future government.

**The Reservists and the Economic Party.** Led by Yoaz Hendel, a former communications minister, and Yaron Zelekha, a former Finance Ministry accountant general. The list is hovering at the threshold: four seats in four of the seven final polls and 1.9–2.8% in the other three.

**Amcha Yisrael (People of Israel).** A right-wing list led by retired IDF general Ofer Winter. It pledges to serve only in a Netanyahu-led government and is also near the threshold: four seats in two of the seven final polls and 1.5–3.2% in the rest. On 19 October a car carrying Fleur Hassan-Nahoum, No. 5 on its list, was hit by a rock in the West Bank town of Turmus Ayya. Winter accused the government of neglecting the fight against terrorism.

**Blue and White.** Led by Benny Gantz, it withdrew on 19 October after polling below the threshold (1.1% in Maariv). Gantz said he would "continue to work for unity". Other lists polling well under 1% include Haredi Public, Noam (Avi Maoz) and Tzomet–Beit Yisrael.

They consistently show the Netanyahu bloc at 62 seats, while every other publisher shows it at 46–58.

**Final Zman Yisrael / Times of Israel poll:**
- Each bloc stands at 51 seats, with the Joint List at 9, Ra'am at 5 and the Reservists at 4 (3.6%).
- Yashar is down three seats from the previous week, to 23. Likud is up one seat, to 21; Trump's endorsement came between the two polls.
- Amcha Yisrael is below the threshold at 3%.
- 87% of respondents have made a final decision. 8.4% are undecided between parties in the same bloc, 2.4% between the blocs, and 2.2% don't know.
- Over 25% changed their minds during the campaign's final months.
- 59.6% say Netanyahu should leave politics if he fails to form a government, against 28.8% who say he should not.

**Who is better suited to be prime minister:**
- **Channel 12 (19–20 Oct):** Eisenkot 45% vs Netanyahu 33%, a gap that widened from 10 points to 12. Bennett 39% vs Netanyahu 34%, with 22% saying neither.
- **Maariv (14–15 Oct):** Eisenkot 48% vs Netanyahu 41%. Bennett and Netanyahu tied at 43%. Netanyahu 43% vs Liberman 37%.

**Effect of Trump's endorsement (Channel 12):** 64% say it makes no difference to their vote. 17% say it makes them less likely to back Likud and 12% more likely.

**Commitment (Walla, 19 Oct):** the share of each party's voters who are completely certain:
- Joint List 81%
- Likud 68%
- RZP–Zehut 62%
- Shas 56%
- Yashar 40%
- Together 23%

Overall, 54% of coalition voters are completely certain, against 35% of opposition voters. The coalition's base is firmer; the opposition's vote is softer and more fluid.

**If there is a deadlock (Maariv):** most respondents prefer a unity government to another election; 27% would rather vote again and 18% are unsure. 54% of opposition voters would back a unity government only if their own bloc leads it. Nearly 40% of coalition voters want a unity government headed by Netanyahu. 79% expect their own bloc to win (90% of coalition voters, 75% of opposition voters).

## 5. The issues driving the vote

- **Accountability for 7 October.** Many voters want to know who is responsible for the failures of 7 October 2023. The change bloc has promised a commission of inquiry. NPR described the election as a verdict on Netanyahu's handling of 7 October.
- **Drafting ultra-Orthodox men.** Years of war put heavy reserve-duty burdens on those who serve, sharpening anger over the exemption most Haredi men still have. The issue broke the coalition: UTJ quit in July 2025. The March 2026 state budget, the largest in Israel's history, sent billions of shekels to Haredi institutions. The change bloc promises a unified position on conscription.
- **Cost of living.** The cost of living is one of voters' top concerns, according to CFR and AFP reporting.
- **Security on several fronts.** A ceasefire is formally in place in Gaza, but deadly Israeli strikes there were still reported on 20 October. Israeli strikes and shelling in southern Lebanon continued the same day. Confrontation with Iran is a central theme: Trump praised Netanyahu for "combatting the Islamic Republic of Iran".
- **Netanyahu's trial and a possible pardon.** The trial continues and the pardon request is unresolved. Trump wrote to President Herzog asking for a pardon and later publicly criticized him for not granting one.
- **The United States and Israel's standing abroad.** Trump's endorsement on 18 October was the first explicit public endorsement of a candidate in an Israeli election by a sitting US president. Before that, relations had been tense: Trump confirmed in June 2026 that he had called Netanyahu "crazy". The endorsement gives Netanyahu an answer to critics who say he has damaged ties with Israel's closest ally. Israel's standing abroad since the Gaza war is itself a campaign issue.
- **Palestinian statehood** is not a priority for most voters. Virtually every Jewish-majority party, including Netanyahu's main challengers, rejects it; only the Arab parties support it.
- **Democracy and governance.** The legacy of the 2023 judicial overhaul still shapes the campaign. That overhaul triggered mass protests, and on 1 January 2024 the Supreme Court struck down the law limiting the "reasonableness" standard by 8–7. The change bloc promises prime-ministerial term limits and a constitution.
- **Arab parties as partners.** Whether Ra'am or the Joint List can be part of, or support, a government is a central dividing line. Likud campaigns hard against it, and most of the opposition has ruled it out.

## 6. Campaign timeline

- **30 Mar 2026:** the Knesset passes the 2026 budget, the largest in Israel's history, with billions for Haredi institutions.
- **May–Jul 2026:** the coalition advances a bill to dissolve the Knesset. On 17 July the Knesset dissolves and the election is set for 27 October.
- **20 Jul:** The Democrats announce their primary results (86% turnout).
- **26 Jul:** the Religious Zionist Party holds primaries, with Smotrich at the head of the slate. Ra'am also holds primaries and says it will run alone.
- **8 Sep:** 38 lists are submitted, including Likud's 35-person slate.
- **September:** the change bloc's leaders sign their joint statement of principles.
- **10 Sep:** surplus-vote agreements between Together and Yisrael Beiteinu and between Yashar and The Democrats.
- **12 Sep:** surplus-vote agreement between the Joint List and Ra'am.
- **27 Sep:** Eisenkot meets hostage families at Hostages Square.
- **29 Sep:** the Shin Bet declines a state security detail for Eisenkot. The Democrats petition the High Court over Golan's protection.
- **September:** Eisenkot challenges Netanyahu to a televised debate, and Likud answers with its "Abbas, Tibi, Golan and Liberman" attack.
- **6 Oct:** surplus-vote agreement between Likud and RZP–Zehut.
- **11 Oct:** surplus-vote agreement between Shas and UTJ, with a pledge to cooperate.
- **Mid–late Oct:** Likud supporter and media personality Yinon Magal makes remarks that are widely denounced as anti-Mizrahi slurs. They are compared to the "chakhchakhim" (1981) and "amulet kissers" (2015) episodes.
- **18 Oct (Sunday):** Trump endorses Netanyahu, calling him "a Great Warrior Prime Minister, and an even greater Partner for Peace in combatting the Islamic Republic of Iran". Netanyahu replies: "Together we will complete the mission!"
- **19 Oct:** Gantz withdraws Blue and White. The Democrats' billboard is vandalized, and the rock attack on the Amcha Yisrael candidate's car in Turmus Ayya takes place.
- **21 Oct:** Yisrael Beiteinu complains to the Knesset sergeant-at-arms about death threats against Liberman.
- **22 Oct:** the final Times of Israel poll shows the blocs tied at 51–51.
- **23 Oct, 23:59:** the poll blackout begins.
- **27 Oct:** election day. Exit polls follow at 22:00.

## 7. Voters, media and the online conversation

**Key voter groups:**
- Secular, liberal voters in the Tel Aviv area and the centre, including veterans of the 2023 protests (Yashar, Together, The Democrats).
- Reservists and their families, who carried the burden of the war (Yashar, the Reservists, Yisrael Beiteinu, Together).
- Traditional and Mizrahi voters in development towns and the periphery (Likud's base; also Shas and Otzma Yehudit).
- National-religious voters and West Bank settlers (RZP–Zehut, Otzma Yehudit, Amcha Yisrael, part of Likud).
- Ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) voters, for whom the draft exemption is the central issue: Mizrahi Haredim mainly vote Shas and Ashkenazi Haredim mainly vote UTJ.
- Secular, Russian-speaking immigrants and their children (Yisrael Beiteinu).
- Arab citizens of Israel, whose turnout is decisive for the Joint List and Ra'am; polls point to record combined support of about 14 seats.
- Families of hostages and 7 October victims, and residents of the Gaza border communities and the north, for whom accountability and security dominate.

**Media:**
- The main TV news channels are Channel 12 (HaHadashot 12), Channel 13, Kan 11 (the public broadcaster), Channel 14 (right-wing and pro-Netanyahu) and i24NEWS.
- Major news sites and papers include Ynet / Yedioth Ahronoth, Walla, Maariv, Israel Hayom, Haaretz, The Times of Israel, The Jerusalem Post and Arutz Sheva.
- Online, the campaign plays out on X, Facebook, TikTok, Telegram and WhatsApp groups. AI-generated campaign videos have become a controversy in this election.
- The campaign environment is tense. Golan, Eisenkot and Liberman have received death threats, and observers warn about incitement and political violence.

## 8. Open questions for election day

1. Can either bloc reach 61 seats without crossing a red line: Arab parties for the opposition, or new partners for Netanyahu?
2. Do the Reservists and Amcha Yisrael cross the 3.25% threshold? Each list that falls short wastes roughly 3% of the vote, which shifts the seat math between the blocs.
3. Where do Blue and White's remaining voters go after Gantz's withdrawal, and how much of its ~1% vote is wasted anyway?
4. Turnout: does Arab turnout reach a record? Does the Haredi vote turn out as usual? How motivated are soft opposition voters compared with the more committed coalition base?
5. Does Trump's endorsement move votes in the final days, or does it backfire with centrist voters?
6. Does UTJ cross over to support an Eisenkot-led government, or return to Netanyahu?
7. After the vote, who receives the mandate? Is the result a rotation or unity government, a narrow government with outside support, or a repeat election?
